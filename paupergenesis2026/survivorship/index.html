Survivorship vs. funnel

Does the selection rule change the metagame?

Why look at the tournament this way?

A survivorship view follows the tournament forward, one round at a time, and asks who remains in contention after each result. I use X-1-1 or better as a simple, consistent proxy for still having a realistic path to prizing. In this model, those players are “alive.” As soon as someone falls below that threshold, they leave the population in every later round, even if they keep playing and improve their final standing. The chart above recalculates the archetype shares using only those still-alive players.

I started thinking about this after hearing a discussion on the Common Ground Pauper Podcast about the three phases of a large tournament. There is the opening field, where everyone and every registered deck is still present; the middle rounds, where players who are still alive increasingly play against other successful decks; and the closing stretch, where only a small group remains in realistic contention. Those phases can feel like different metagames from the player’s seat. A deck that is common at the bottom tables may disappear from the matches that decide prizes, while a strategy with a modest registration share can become a recurring feature of the contending brackets.

I had a fairly personal reason to wonder about it. I won my first three rounds at Paupergenesis, then got snuffed out. The final standings preserve where I landed, but they flatten the part I actually experienced: three rounds of being undefeated, the opponents and decks around me while I was alive, and the point where that version of the tournament ended. A tournament path has a shape that a final rank does not.

This analysis was possible because Paupergenesis was hosted on Melee.gg with public individual match results, round-by-round standings, and decklists. That level of transparency let me reconstruct who each player faced, what their record was after every round, and exactly when they crossed a chosen threshold. Most published tournament data offers only a field and a finish. Here, I can watch the field change while the event is still happening.

The funnel I published in my first Paupergenesis report measures one familiar version of that story by taking the final standings and asking which archetypes occupy the Top 64, Top 32, and Top 16. That is useful, but it reads the tournament backward from its final result. Survivorship asks a different question: what did the metagame look like among the players who were still alive at that moment? It is deliberately a rough model of continued contention rather than an attempt to reproduce the tournament organizer’s exact prize math.

Some drift between the two views is intuitive. They begin with the same full field and, in this event, end with the same Top 16, so the middle rounds are where the populations have room to separate. What interests me is not simply whether they drift, but how far. The distance grows from 7.3% after Round 2 to 12.6% after Round 4, then jumps to 23.1% after Round 5 and peaks at 32.3% after Round 6. That Round 6 population contains 31 players, almost exactly the funnel’s Top 32 checkpoint, yet the two groups are at their most different; matching headcount does not mean matching membership. The distance falls to 15.8% after Round 7 before returning to zero after Round 8.

To measure that distance, I ask how much of one archetype mix would have to change before it matched the other. A 32.3% difference in a 31-player group means replacing roughly 10 of those 31 deck slots with different archetypes. At the Round 6 snapshot, for example, Mono Blue Terror occupies three seats in the final Top 31 but none in the X-1-1 population, while Mono Red Rally has three players alive compared with one in the final Top 31.

That middle-round gap sounds dramatic, but groups this small can look very different just through ordinary variation. To check whether 32.3% was genuinely unusual, I randomly reassigned the archetype labels among the same players 10,000 times while leaving membership in the two groups unchanged. Those random versions averaged a 29.9% difference, and a gap at least as large as the real one appeared about 44% of the time. No round produced a difference rare enough to separate it clearly from chance. So this event does not show strong evidence of a distinct hidden metagame. What survivorship does give me is a different and, arguably, more player-shaped description of the tournament: which decks were still in the room around me while the event was being decided. Repeating the analysis across more large events would tell me whether the apparent three-phase pattern is durable or simply the normal volatility of small late-round fields.

Chapter two · Genesis vs. Geddon

Was Paupergeddon more settled?

While exploring the Paupergeddon Summer 2026 lists, I had the impression that decks carrying the same archetype label were packed more tightly together. The feeling was especially strong in Mono Red Madness. My Paupergenesis list used two Sazacap’s Brew and two Thermo-Alchemist, but those choices seemed to vanish in the much larger Italian field. That raised a second question: two tournaments can have similar archetype shares, but did one of them leave less room for invention inside those archetypes?

I measured every pair of main decks within each shared archetype using Bray-Curtis distance on the card counts. Because these are effectively 60-card lists, I can translate the result into a more intuitive number: roughly how many card slots differ between two randomly chosen lists. I included the 15 archetypes with at least five decks at both events, treated snow-covered basics as their ordinary counterparts, and combined the same functional Pauper cards used by my deck explorer.

The first impression is directionally right, but not universal. Paupergeddon was tighter in nine of the 15 shared archetypes, and the typical archetype moved only 0.2 card slots closer together. Some changes were substantial: White Weenie lists averaged 13.4 slots apart at Genesis and 7.3 at Geddon, while Dimir Terror fell from 10.1 to 5.7. Other decks moved the opposite way. Jeskai Ephemerate rose from 6.5 to 10.1 slots apart, and Spy Combo from 5.4 to 9.0. This looks less like one event being uniformly more solved and more like individual archetypes reaching different stages of convergence.

Mono Red Madness shows only a small shift. Its average pair moved from 3.2 slots apart at Genesis to 2.9 at Geddon, and 76% of Geddon lists landed within two slots of the event’s central list, compared with 69% at Genesis. Sazacap’s Brew appeared in four of 26 Genesis lists (15.4%) but only three of 132 Geddon lists (2.3%). Thermo-Alchemist appeared twice at Genesis and not once at Geddon. At the same time, Geddon developed a different branch: Melded Moxite appeared in 27 lists after being absent from Genesis. Convergence did not mean perfect agreement; it meant a denser stock shell with a few organized departures.

I use that projection as a map, not as the measurement itself: UMAP is excellent at showing neighborhoods, but the card-slot distances in the comparison chart are calculated directly from the full decklists. Do stock lists survive longer?

The comparison raised an obvious follow-up: does staying close to the established list improve a player’s chances of remaining alive? For each of the 170 Paupergenesis decks in these archetypes, I measured the distance from its archetype’s central list and ranked it only against decks with the same label. That keeps this from becoming a disguised comparison of strong and weak archetypes. I then reused the X-1-1 survivorship score from the first chapter.

I found essentially no relationship. The within-archetype rank correlation between stockness and the last round alive is -0.02, on a scale from -1 to 1. When I shuffled survival results among players in the same archetype 10,000 times, a relationship at least that large appeared 84% of the time. The most stock group remained alive for 3.2 rounds on average, compared with 3.0 for the least stock group; after Round 4, the difference was 36.5% versus 32.6%. Those are small gaps, and the curves do not separate consistently.

This does not prove that card choices never matter. One tournament leaves plenty of room for a small effect, and decklist distance cannot capture whether an unusual card was a sharp metagame call or simply a bad idea. Pilot skill, matchup order, and familiarity with the stock list are tangled into the result too. The narrower conclusion is that Paupergenesis gives me no evidence that registering a more conventional list, by itself, produced a higher survival score.
